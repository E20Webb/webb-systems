---

During most of my twenties and early thirties, I worked in high-pressure sales for two Fortune 500 companies.

I was not responsible for national advertising.

I was responsible for something more immediate:

making sure people knew who I was, what I sold, and why they should care.

Once a week, I kept a fishbowl beside the cash register at a local restaurant.

Diners could drop in a business card or fill out a small raffle slip for a free lunch.

Over my own lunch break, I would come in, get the roomâ€™s attention, give a fifteen-second pitch, and draw names until I found someone who was actually there.

It worked because the giveaway created a reason to pay attention.

The pitch worked because it was brief.

The entire thing worked because it fit naturally into the environment instead of fighting it.

I used the same instinct everywhere else.

I mailed letters to leads I could not reach by phone.

Years later, in e-commerce, I learned how to advertise on TikTok without breaking the TikTok loop.

Different medium.

Same rule.

**If you werenâ€™t advertising, you werenâ€™t selling.**

But there was a second rule that mattered just as much:

**If the advertising broke the experience, you were doing it badly.**

That is what I keep thinking about when I look at console and PC gaming.

Gaming platforms have enormous amounts of attention.

But comparatively few ways to monetize that attention outside:

- storefronts
- subscriptions
- discounts
- DLC
- live-service monetization
- the games themselves

A discount tile does not create desire.

A marketplace does not automatically create discovery.

And a launcher cannot assume that because the customer arrived, the sale has already been earned.

In sales, I was never afforded the luxury of waiting for money to appear.

I had to pursue attention strategically.

I had to earn the business.

Gaming platforms should think the same way.

---

# THE WRONG QUESTION

The industry usually asks:

**How do we put more ads into games?**

That is the wrong question.

Players already know why they hate advertising in games.

It interrupts.

It delays.

It breaks immersion.

It often appears at the exact moment the player wants to do something else.

Traditional games are built around:

- pacing
- immersion
- agency
- progression
- tension
- reward
- continuity

Advertising inserted into those experiences feels foreign because it is foreign.

The game was not designed around it.

The ad was bolted on afterward.

```text
TRADITIONAL GAME
    +
RETROFITTED AD
    =
INTERRUPTION
```

That is not an advertising failure.

It is an architecture failure.

The problem is not that advertising exists.

The problem is that the revenue model does not belong to the mode of play.

---

# ADVERTISING IS A MODE PROBLEM

Different experiences can support different economic models.

Premium games do not need to behave like mobile games.

Competitive environments do not need to monetize like story campaigns.

Social feeds do not need to monetize like storefronts.

Short-form discovery does not need to monetize like a sixty-hour RPG.

The industry keeps trying to force a small set of business models across every type of experience.

That creates friction.

A better approach is structural:

**design the mode of play and the economic model together.**

```text
NEW MODE OF PLAY
    +
NATIVE ECONOMIC MODEL
    =
REVENUE WITHOUT DISRUPTION
```

That means the answer is not more advertising inside traditional games.

The answer is creating new play surfaces where advertising belongs.

---

# VALUE BEFORE ATTENTION

Mobile advertising taught an entire generation a new interaction grammar.

Short.

Swipeable.

Immediate.

Playable.

Disposable.

Familiar within seconds.

The problem is not the grammar.

The problem is how often that grammar is used coercively.

The ad interrupts.

The demo misrepresents the game.

The close button moves.

The reward is withheld.

The player is forced to sample something they did not ask for.

The lesson should not be:

**copy mobile ads.**

The lesson should be:

**separate the interaction grammar from the punishment.**

Younger players already understand:

- rapid sampling
- short-form discovery
- progression
- rewards
- seasonal structures
- instant transitions

Those behaviors can support a much better model.

One simple rule changes everything:

**Give the player value before asking for attention.**

A player sees:

- a trailer
- a store tile
- a recommendation
- a discount
- a review

The economic principle is simple:

**Monetize curiosity, not frustration.**

Traditional mobile ads often interrupt one game to advertise another.

A better system turns discovery itself into something worth choosing.

**The best ad surface may be an experience worth playing.**

# TRADITIONAL GAMES SHOULD BE LEFT ALONE

This is the part that should be simple.

Traditional players do not want:

- interruptions
- forced demos
- mobile-style coercion
- ads inserted into premium experiences
- monetization systems that distort difficulty
- progression designed around frustration

They should not get any of that.

Premium games should remain premium games.

Story-driven games should protect pacing.

Competitive games should protect competitive integrity.

Single-player games should protect immersion.

Existing experiences should not be degraded to support a new revenue model.

The platform should create new surfaces instead.

**New revenue should come from new modes of play, not from breaking old ones.**

---

# THE RULES

If advertising becomes part of a gaming platform, the architecture should enforce clear boundaries.

## 1. NEVER INTERRUPT HIGH-INTENSITY PLAY

Advertising belongs at natural boundaries.

Not during action.

Not during emotional peaks.

Not between input and response.

## 2. VALUE COMES FIRST

The player should receive meaningful entertainment, discovery, or utility before attention is monetized.

## 3. ADS SHOULD BE LEGIBLE

Advertising should not depend on deception.

The player should understand when something is sponsored.

## 4. MONETIZATION SHOULD NOT DISTORT GAMEPLAY

Difficulty should not increase to create ad demand.

Progression should not be deliberately slowed to sell relief.

## 5. THE PLAYER SHOULD HAVE A CLEAN EXIT

Ad-supported experiences should not depend on traps.

Participation should remain voluntary.

## 6. ATTENTION IS A FINITE RESOURCE

More ads do not automatically create more value.

Saturation destroys the surface.

## 7. THE MODE SHOULD JUSTIFY THE MODEL

If advertising feels foreign to the experience, the architecture is wrong.

**commerce**
