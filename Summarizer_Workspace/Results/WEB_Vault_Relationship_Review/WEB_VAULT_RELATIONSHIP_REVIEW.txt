0088: 
0089: YouTube became an archive of guides, personalities, criticism, speedruns, history, memes, discoveries, failures, and cultural memory.
0090: 
0091: None of these systems replaced the game.
0092: 
0093: They filled needs that existed around it.
0094: 
0095: And more recently, platforms such as Roblox and Fortnite have begun recombining functions that had separated: play, creation, identity, commerce, events, social presence, and user-generated experiences can increasingly exist within the same broad environment.
0096: 
0097: These systems are radically different from one another.
0098: 
0099: They should not be treated as stages of one inevitable platform evolution.
0100: 
0101: But they repeatedly answer the same underlying demand:
0102: 
0103: **people do not only want games.**
0104: 
0105: They build places around games.
0106: 
0107: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0108:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0109:     II.
0110:   </div>
0111: 
0112:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0113:     Play Is Relational
0114:   </h2>
0115: </div>
0116: 
0117: The scale of that behavior is not trivial.
0118: 
0119: The Entertainment Software Association's 2024 U.S. research reported that 74 percent of players play with other people online or in person, while 55 percent play with others weekly. Fifty-five percent agreed that games can introduce people to new friends.
0120: 
0121: Its 2025 research reported that 55 percent of players still played with others weekly, while 78 percent agreed that games could introduce them to new friends and 49 percent said games had helped them stay connected with friends and family.
0122: 
0123: That does not mean every player wants every game to become social.
0124: 
0125: It means something more basic:
0126: 
0127: **gaming is substantially relational.**
0128: 
0129: Academic research points in the same direction, while adding useful limits.
0130: 
0131: A 2025 systematic review examining 22 studies of MMOs found that collective play, game involvement, and participation in in-game communities were generally associated with positive social-capital outcomes. It also found diminishing returns: excessive amounts of play could correlate negatively with social capital.
0132: 
0133: Earlier longitudinal research similarly found that collective MMORPG play positively influenced forms of online bonding and bridging social capital, while failing to demonstrate that all of those effects carried into offline relationships.
0134: 
0135: That nuance matters.
0136: 
0137: The argument is not that online games automatically produce friendship, healthy communities, or meaningful belonging.
0138: 
0139: The argument is that **collective play creates conditions in which social value can emerge**.
0140: 
0141: And once that value exists, players need somewhere for it to persist.
0142: 
0143: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0144:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0145:     III.
0146:   </div>
0147: 
0148:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">

0150:   </h2>
0151: </div>
0152: 
0153: It would be easy to take this argument too far.
0154: 
0155: Culture does not belong exclusively to the shared place.
0156: 
0157: Games create culture.
0158: 
0159: A fighting game can produce its own competitive language and rituals.
0160: 
0161: A role-playing game can produce attachment to characters and worlds.
0162: 
0163: A franchise can accumulate decades of memory.
0164: 
0165: A creator can build a community larger than many games.
0166: 
0167: A guild can become more important to its members than the MMO in which it first formed.
0168: 
0169: A studio can develop an identity recognizable across completely different products.
0170: 
0171: These are not interchangeable pieces waiting for a platform to give them meaning.
0172: 
0173: They arrive with meaning of their own.
0174: 
0175: That suggests a more useful distinction:
0176: 
0177: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0178:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">

0180:   </p>
0181: </div>
0182:

0184: 
0185: It created a context in which the person playing <em>Street Fighter</em> could encounter another player, become known, discover another game, observe another community, or return next week and find part of the social environment still there.
0186: 
0187: Discord does not create the games discussed inside its servers.
0188: 
0189: Steam does not create most of the games in its library.
0190: 
0191: YouTube does not create most of the cultural events its videos preserve.
0192: 
0193: Their value comes partly from giving independent cultures additional surfaces on which to exist.
0194: 
0195: That is different from ownership.
0196: 
0197: And it becomes important once the culture around gaming starts spreading across more places than any one game can contain.
0198: 
0199: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0200:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0201:     IV.
0202:   </div>
0203: 
0204:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0205:     Where the Culture Went
0206:   </h2>
0207: </div>
0208: 
0209: Consider the experience surrounding a single modern game.
0210: 
0211: The game may provide the play.
0212: 
0213: The console or PC platform may provide identity, ownership, achievements, and multiplayer services.
0214: 
0215: Discord may hold the persistent community.
0216: 
0217: Twitch may provide live spectatorship.
0218: 
0219: YouTube may hold guides, history, criticism, highlights, and cultural memory.
0220: 
0221: Reddit or another social network may host discussion.
0222: 
0223: A separate service may host modifications.
0224: 
0225: Tournament infrastructure may exist somewhere else.
0226: 
0227: Statistics may live on a third-party website.
0228: 
0229: Creators may maintain communities of their own.
0230: 
0231: None of this is inherently dysfunctional.
0232: 
0233: In fact, much of it exists because specialized products became better at solving specific problems than the game or platform itself.
0234: 
0235: Discord did not become important to gaming merely because console manufacturers forgot to add another menu.
0236: 
0237: It built a product people wanted to use.
0238: 
0239: YouTube is not valuable to gaming because games failed to include enough video.
0240: 
0241: Twitch is not simply a missing Xbox feature.
0242: 
0243: Specialization creates value.
0244: 
0245: So the problem is not that gaming culture became distributed.
0246: 
0247: The more interesting problem is that **continuity often did not travel with it**.
0248: 
0249: A person's identity in one place may have little meaning in another.
0250: 
0251: A community may live separately from the activity around which it formed.
0252: 
0253: Discovery may happen in one system while ownership happens in another.
0254: 
0255: Spectatorship can produce interest without producing a clean path to participation.
0256: 
0257: Relationships that matter to play may be invisible to the platform where play actually happens.
0258: 
0259: The pieces work.
0260: 
0261: The connections between the pieces remain inconsistent.
0262: 
0263: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0264:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0265:     V.
0266:   </div>
0267: 
0268:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">

0270:   </h2>
0271: </div>
0272: 
0273: Discord may be the clearest example of why that distinction matters.

0276:

0278: 
0279: That mistakes ownership for architecture.
0280:

0286: 
0287: The more important observation is structural.
0288:

0290: 
0291: And the game does not have to become Discord for Discord's relationships to become relevant inside it.
0292:

0294: 
0295: **persistent social infrastructure can remain sovereign while becoming more connected to play.**
0296: 
0297: This is a very different future from putting every function inside one enormous application.
0298: 
0299: It suggests that what gaming lacks may not be one destination that owns everything.
0300: 
0301: It may be an architecture through which different destinations can become more continuous.

0318:

0320:

0322:

0324:

0326: 
0327: You know who may be there.

0383:     VII.
0384:   </div>

0389: </div>
0390: 
0391: This creates a strange condition.

0394:

0396: 
0397: The games exist.
0398:

0422: 
0423: Some boundaries are useful.
0424:

0426:

0444:

0446:

0448:

0450: 
0451: If that can happen, the destination is no longer one application.
0452: 
0453: It becomes the connective environment between applications.
0454: 
0455: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0456:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">

0463: </div>
0464: 
0465: There is an obvious danger in romanticizing the destination.
0466:

0468: 
0469: A system that coordinates identity can also control identity.
0470: 
0471: A system that improves discovery can also determine visibility.
0472: 
0473: A system that connects communities can also moderate them.
0474:

0482:

0486:

0493: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">

0504:

0506: 
0507: Services remain services.
0508: 
0509: The shared layer gives them additional ways to encounter one another.
0510: 
0511: That is closer to a city than a shopping mall.
0512: 
0513: The city creates common infrastructure.
0514: 
0515: It does not require every building to become the same building.
0516: 
0517: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">

0520:   </div>

0525: </div>
0526: 
0527: Gaming has spent decades becoming easier to access while becoming harder to locate culturally.
0528: 
0529: The activity can happen almost anywhere.
0530: 
0531: The culture happens everywhere.
0532: 
0533: That has created extraordinary freedom.
0534:

0536:

0538:

0566:

0134:   </div>
0135: 
0136:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0137:     Why the Board Can Create Value
0138:   </h2>
0139: </div>
0140: 
0141: The phrase <em>network effects</em> is often used as though it explains everything.
0142: 
0143: It does not.
0144: 
0145: There are several different mechanisms through which a shared environment can become economically valuable, and they should not be blurred together.
0146: 
0147: ### Coordination Value
0148: 
0149: The board allows independent participants to find and interact with one another.
0150: 
0151: Players find games.
0152: 
0153: Players find other players.
0154: 
0155: Creators find audiences.
0156: 
0157: Communities find members.
0158: 
0159: Developers find distribution.
0160: 
0161: Services find demand.
0162: 
0163: The board creates value by reducing the friction between participants who would otherwise have to find one another independently.
0164: 
0165: ### Continuity Value
0166: 
0167: A persistent environment can carry information forward.
0168: 
0169: Identity.
0170: 
0171: Relationships.
0172: 
0173: Purchases.
0174: 
0175: History.
0176: 
0177: Reputation.
0178: 
0179: Preferences.
0180: 
0181: Achievements.
0182: 
0183: Communities.
0184: 
0185: Entitlements.
0186: 
0187: The participant does not begin from zero every time the activity changes.
0188: 
0189: That matters because accumulated context can make the next interaction more valuable than the first.
0190: 
0191: ### Distribution Value
0192: 
0193: Once participation already exists, new activity can be distributed through the environment.
0194: 
0195: A game can be discovered because a friend is playing it.
0196: 
0197: A creator can surface because a community is already active.
0198: 
0199: An old game can become relevant because something elsewhere in the ecosystem points back toward it.
0200: 
0201: Distribution becomes less dependent on attracting every participant from outside the system each time.
0202: 
0203: ### Cross-Side Value
0204: 
0205: More players can make the platform more attractive to creators and developers.
0206: 
0207: More creators and developers can make the platform more attractive to players.
0208: 
0209: This is the basic dynamic at the center of two-sided platform economics.
0210: 
0211: ### Transaction Value
0212: 
0213: Economic activity is easier where participation, identity, trust, payments, and discovery already exist.
0214: 
0215: That can include:
0216: 
0217: games,
0218: 
0219: services,
0220: 
0221: marketplaces,
0222: 
0223: subscriptions,
0224: 
0225: creator activity,
0226: 
0227: events,
0228: 
0229: digital goods,
0230: 
0231: advertising,
0232: 
0233: or other forms of commerce.
0234: 
0235: The point is not that every possible transaction should be monetized.
0236: 
0237: The point is that the board can create a common commercial layer around activity already occurring.
0238: 
0239: ### Risk-Reduction Value
0240: 
0241: There is another form of value that is less glamorous but potentially just as important.
0242: 
0243: Operating a platform requires solving difficult problems repeatedly:
0244: 
0245: payments,
0246: 
0247: fraud,
0248: 
0249: account security,
0250: 
0251: parental controls,
0252: 
0253: identity,
0254: 
0255: moderation,
0256: 
0257: trust and safety,
0258: 
0259: compliance,
0260: 
0261: global distribution,
0262: 
0263: support,
0264: 
0265: data handling,
0266: 
0267: and dispute resolution.
0268: 
0269: If every publisher rebuilds those systems independently, the industry reproduces the same infrastructure again and again.
0270: 
0271: A shared platform can potentially turn some of those duplicated burdens into common infrastructure.
0272: 
0273: That creates value even before another player arrives.
0274: 
0275: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0276:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0277:     A board can become valuable not only because more people are on it, but because it makes participation cheaper, safer, and easier to operate.
0278:   </p>
0279: </div>
0280: 
0281: That may be one of the most important forms of leverage available to a platform.
0282: 
0283: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0284:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0285:     III.
0286:   </div>
0287: 
0288:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0289:     Steam Already Demonstrates Part of the Thesis
0290:   </h2>
0291: </div>
0292: 
0293: Steam is often described as a storefront.
0294: 
0295: That description is incomplete.
0296: 
0297: Steamworks gives third-party developers access to common infrastructure around authentication, distribution, community features, commerce, Workshop integration, cloud functionality, matchmaking, achievements, inventory systems, and other services.
0298: 
0299: The strategic point is simple:
0300: 
0301: most of the games benefiting from those systems are not owned by Valve.
0302: 
0303: Steam demonstrates that a platform can create value by making thousands of independent products easier to distribute, operate, discover, update, monetize, and participate around.
0304: 
0305: Valve does not need to make every game for Steam to become more useful when those games succeed.
0306: 
0307: The games remain the pieces.
0308: 
0309: Steam provides part of the board.
0310: 
0311: That is why the word <em>storefront</em> understates the position.
0312: 
0313: A store helps people buy products.
0314: 
0315: A platform can help products become easier to operate, easier to find, easier to buy, and more valuable because they exist inside a larger system.
0316: 
0317: The difference is not semantic.
0318: 
0319: It is economic.
0320: 
0321: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0322:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0323:     IV.
0324:   </div>
0325: 
0326:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0327:     When the Pieces Win, the Board Wins
0328:   </h2>
0329: </div>
0330: 
0331: The strongest board does not simply collect rent from successful pieces.
0332: 
0333: Its economics improve when the pieces themselves become more valuable.
0334: 
0335: Fortnite's creator economy provides a useful example.
0336: 
0337: Epic's Creator Economy 2.0 places 40 percent of qualifying Fortnite net revenue into an engagement pool for eligible island publishers. The payout system explicitly considers whether an experience attracts new players or brings lapsed players back.
0338: 
0339: The structure matters.
0340: 
0341: A creator produces something valuable.
0342: 
0343: That thing gives someone another reason to enter or return to Fortnite.
0344: 
0345: Fortnite becomes more useful because the creator succeeded.
0346: 
0347: Epic then shares part of the resulting economic value back with the creator.
0348: 
0349: The creator and the board are not economically identical.
0350: 
0351: But they are not purely adversarial either.
0352: 
0353: The model works best when the creator's success increases the value of the larger environment.
0354: 
0355: That suggests a broader principle:
0356: 
0357: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0358:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0359:     The board should be designed so that its best economic outcome comes from making the pieces stronger.
0360:   </p>
0361: </div>
0362: 
0363: That is more durable than maximizing the percentage extracted from every transaction.
0364: 
0365: If the board makes participants more successful, their activity can create more reasons for everyone else to participate.
0366: 
0367: More games.
0368: 
0369: More creators.
0370: 
0371: More communities.
0372: 
0373: More transactions.
0374: 
0375: More discovery.
0376: 
0377: More re-engagement.
0378: 
0379: More cultural activity.
0380: 
0381: The board benefits because the environment becomes more useful.
0382: 
0383: That is fundamentally different from a tollbooth.
0384: 
0385: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0386:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0387:     V.
0388:   </div>
0389: 
0390:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0391:     Roblox: Opportunity and Warning
0392:   </h2>
0393: </div>
0394: 
0395: Roblox is one of the strongest examples of the opportunity.
0396: 
0397: It is also one of the strongest warnings against romanticizing it.
0398: 
0399: In 2025, Roblox reported approximately $4.89 billion in revenue, more than $1.50 billion in developer exchange fees, roughly $1.15 billion in infrastructure and trust-and-safety expense, and an operating loss of about $1.23 billion.
0400: 
0401: Those numbers should change the way the board is discussed.
0402: 
0403: Roblox demonstrates that a shared platform can support enormous economic activity generated by outside creators.
0404: 
0405: But it also demonstrates that operating the environment is expensive.
0406: 
0407: The board inherits responsibilities the pieces do not necessarily have to solve at the same scale.
0408: 
0409: Infrastructure.
0410: 
0411: Moderation.
0412: 
0413: Safety.
0414: 
0415: Fraud.
0416: 
0417: Payments.
0418: 
0419: Compliance.
0420: 
0421: Creator payouts.
0422: 
0423: Discovery.
0424: 
0425: Governance.
0426: 
0427: Data centers.
0428: 
0429: Support.
0430: 
0431: The board may reduce creative risk by allowing thousands of independent participants to create experiences.
0432: 
0433: But it assumes coordination risk.
0434: 
0435: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0436:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0437:     The board can externalize some creative risk while internalizing infrastructure, governance, safety, and ecosystem risk.
0438:   </p>
0439: </div>
0440: 
0441: That is not a reason to avoid the position.
0442: 
0443: It is a reason to understand its real economics.
0444: 
0445: The useful CFO question is therefore not:
0446: 
0447: **Does being the board create activity?**
0448: 
0449: Clearly it can.
0450: 
0451: The question is:
0452: 
0453: **Does the value created by the shared environment exceed the cost of operating it?**
0454: 
0455: That answer will vary by platform.
0456: 
0457: There is no automatic network-effect escape hatch from operating discipline.
0458: 
0459: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0460:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0461:     VI.
0462:   </div>
0463: 
0464:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0465:     The Launcher Question
0466:   </h2>
0467: </div>
0468: 
0469: This brings the argument closer to the existing structure of the industry.
0470: 
0471: Why does a publisher build a launcher?
0472: 
0473: Usually not because another launcher was missing from the world.
0474: 
0475: A launcher can give the publisher:
0476: 
0477: direct customer access,
0478: 
0479: better transaction economics,
0480: 
0481: control over identity,
0482: 
0483: control over distribution,
0484: 
0485: access to data,
0486: 
0487: promotional surfaces,
0488: 
0489: community presence,
0490: 
0491: strategic independence,
0492: 
0493: and leverage against another platform.
0494: 
0495: Those are rational goals.
0496: 
0497: If a publisher maintains its own platform layer, the right response is not to say that it should stop.
0498: 
0499: The better question is:
0500: 
0501: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0502:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0503:     What would a shared board have to provide for maintaining a separate launcher to create less incremental value than participating deeply on the board?
0504:   </p>
0505: </div>
0506: 
0507: That is a much higher bar.
0508: 
0509: The board has to compete not just on audience size.
0510: 
0511: It has to compete on economics.
0512: 
0513: Infrastructure.
0514: 
0515: Control.
0516: 
0517: Trust.
0518: 
0519: Distribution.
0520: 
0521: Discovery.
0522: 
0523: Flexibility.
0524: 
0525: And sovereignty.
0526: 
0527: A publisher will not abandon independent infrastructure simply because another platform asks nicely.
0528: 
0529: The board has to make redundancy unattractive.
0530: 
0531: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0532:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0533:     VII.
0534:   </div>
0535: 
0536:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0537:     Pay for the Board
0538:   </h2>
0539: </div>
0540: 
0541: Most digital stores rely heavily on transaction percentages.
0542: 
0543: That model is simple.
0544: 
0545: The platform takes a share of revenue in exchange for distribution and infrastructure.
0546: 
0547: But it is not the only possible model.
0548: 
0549: Epic already demonstrates how far platform economics have moved from the old universal 30 percent assumption. Its store currently offers developers 100 percent of the first $1 million in annual net revenue per title, then reverts to an 88/12 developer-platform split.
0550: 
0551: That raises the competitive bar for any new board.
0552: 
0553: It cannot merely say:
0554: 
0555: **We take less than the old stores.**
0556: 
0557: It has to offer something structurally better.
0558: 
0559: One possibility is to separate **platform access** from **transaction taxation**.
0560: 
0561: A publisher might pay for infrastructure directly.
0562: 
0563: Smaller participants could still use a conventional revenue-share model.
0564: 
0565: Larger publishers could pay an annual platform subscription or negotiated infrastructure fee in exchange for lower marginal transaction costs.
0566: 
0567: Strategic participants could have custom agreements reflecting the scale of infrastructure, distribution, commerce, and support they consume.
0568: 
0569: The specific percentages would require modeling.
0570: 
0571: But the principle is important:
0572: 
0573: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0574:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0575:     Pay for the board. Keep more of what you earn on it.
0576:   </p>
0577: </div>
0578: 
0579: That changes the economic relationship.
0580: 
0581: The platform no longer has to maximize the percentage it takes from every successful piece.
0582: 
0583: It can make money because the participant chooses to use the infrastructure.
0584: 
0585: That creates a different CFO comparison.
0586: 
0587: Not:
0588: 
0589: **Is 12 percent better than 30 percent?**
0590: 
0591: But:
0592: 
0593: **What does it cost us to operate independently, and what additional value do we gain by operating here?**
0594: 
0595: That comparison includes costs such as:
0596: 
0597: distribution systems,
0598: 
0599: payment infrastructure,
0600: 
0601: global commerce,
0602: 
0603: customer support,
0604: 
0605: fraud prevention,
0606: 
0607: account recovery,
0608: 
0609: moderation,
0610: 
0611: parental controls,
0612: 
0613: identity systems,
0614: 
0615: social systems,
0616: 
0617: discovery,
0618: 
0619: analytics,
0620: 
0621: technical maintenance,
0622: 
0623: and compliance.
0624: 
0625: The board wins if its combined cost and value proposition is better than rebuilding those systems independently.
0626: 
0627: That is platform substitution.
0628: 
0629: And unlike simple storefront competition, it can create advantages even for a publisher that already has an audience.
0630: 
0631: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0632:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0633:     VIII.
0634:   </div>
0635: 
0636:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0637:     The Board Must Create More Than It Takes
0638:   </h2>
0639: </div>
0640: 
0641: Every platform extracts value.
0642: 
0643: Servers cost money.
0644: 
0645: Safety costs money.
0646: 
0647: Development costs money.
0648: 
0649: Support costs money.
0650: 
0651: Investors expect returns.
0652: 
0653: The relevant distinction is not extraction versus purity.
0654: 
0655: It is **value extraction versus value creation**.
0656: 
0657: A weak platform asks:
0658: 
0659: **How much can we take from the activity already happening here?**
0660: 
0661: A stronger platform asks:
0662: 
0663: **How much more activity can participants create because the platform exists?**
0664: 
0665: The difference matters.
0666: 
0667: If the board improves distribution, participants can reach people they could not efficiently reach alone.
0668: 
0669: If it reduces fraud, participants lose less value.
0670: 
0671: If it supplies compliance infrastructure, participants avoid duplicated operating costs.
0672: 
0673: If it carries identity and relationships across experiences, users have more reasons to remain active.
0674: 
0675: If it improves discovery, older or smaller pieces can find new demand.
0676: 
0677: If it makes commerce easier, more transactions can occur.
0678: 
0679: Then the platform is not merely dividing existing value.
0680: 
0681: It is helping increase the total amount of value available to divide.
0682: 
0683: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0684:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0685:     The best board economics come from increasing the size of the game, not merely increasing the platform's share of the pot.
0686:   </p>
0687: </div>
0688: 
0689: That is the alignment problem.
0690: 
0691: When the publisher wins on the board, the board should win.
0692: 
0693: When the creator wins, the board should become more useful.
0694: 
0695: When the community becomes stronger, the platform should become more valuable.
0696: 
0697: If the board becomes richer while its participants become weaker, the architecture is probably drifting toward extraction.
0698: 
0699: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0700:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0701:     IX.
0702:   </div>
0703: 
0704:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0705:     Having the Pieces Is Not the Same as Having the Board
0706:   </h2>
0707: </div>
0708: 
0709: This distinction becomes especially important when looking at Xbox.
0710: 
0711: Microsoft already possesses many of the things a platform might appear to need.
0712: 
0713: A persistent identity.
0714: 
0715: A social graph.
0716: 
0717: Achievements.
0718: 
0719: Commerce.
0720: 
0721: Subscriptions.
0722: 
0723: A game library.
0724: 
0725: First-party studios.
0726: 
0727: Third-party distribution.
0728: 
0729: Cloud infrastructure.
0730: 
0731: Console hardware.
0732: 
0733: PC distribution.
0734: 
0735: Mobile access.
0736: 
0737: A growing handheld presence through Windows.
0738: 
0739: The problem is not simply that Xbox lacks features.
0740: 
0741: The deeper question is whether those features cohere into the environment where the player's gaming life happens.
0742: 
0743: That is not the same thing.
0744: 
0745: A player can have years of Xbox history and still choose another platform as the center of their future gaming activity.
0746: 
0747: Consider a specific migration case.
0748: 
0749: A player spends years primarily on Xbox console.
0750: 
0751: They accumulate purchases.
0752: 
0753: Achievements.
0754: 
0755: Friends.
0756:

0758: 
0759: Familiarity.
0760: 
0761: Possibly a substantial digital library.
0762:

0764:

0766: 
0767: Their accumulated platform history may be overwhelmingly Xbox.
0768:

0770: 
0771: Nor is accumulated identity.
0772: 
0773: Nor is default presence.
0774: 
0775: The hypothesis worth testing is more structural:
0776: 
0777: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0778:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0779:     A player can bring more history with them from Xbox and still build their future on Steam.
0780:   </p>
0781: </div>
0782: 
0783: That would mean Xbox has continuity without necessarily having destination value.
0784: 
0785: Steam's advantage would not simply be that it knows the player better.
0786: 
0787: It would be that more of the surrounding PC gaming ecosystem converges there.
0788: 
0789: Games.
0790: 
0791: Publishers.
0792: 
0793: Reviews.
0794: 
0795: Mods.
0796: 
0797: Community discussion.
0798: 
0799: Wishlists.
0800: 
0801: Discovery.
0802: 
0803: Friends.
0804: 
0805: Developer updates.
0806: 
0807: Marketplace activity.
0808: 
0809: Player-created content.
0810: 
0811: The important difference is not merely how many features exist.
0812: 
0813: It is how many meaningful relationships among participants are expressed through the same environment.
0814: 
0815: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0816:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0817:     A board is not a checklist of features. It is a system in which the features, participants, and cultures become more valuable in relation to one another.
0818:   </p>
0819: </div>
0820: 
0821: That distinction also changes the way Xbox's cross-device strategy should be evaluated.
0822: 
0823: Microsoft reported in fiscal 2025 that gaming revenue rose 9 percent, Xbox content and services revenue rose 16 percent, and Xbox hardware revenue fell 25 percent.
0824: 
0825: Those figures do not prove that hardware is becoming less important.
0826: 
0827: They describe the current proposition.
0828: 
0829: And the current proposition does not yet include the board described here.
0830: 
0831: That matters because there is no basis for assuming that hardware attached to a stronger ecosystem would behave the same way.
0832: 
0833: The board could make the box more valuable.
0834: 
0835: A console can offer strong performance, convenience, reliability, and price relative to a gaming PC built strictly for play.
0836: 
0837: If users still migrate toward other devices or other environments, the lesson should not automatically be that the box is obsolete.
0838: 
0839: It may mean the environment around the box is not compelling enough.
0840: 
0841: The same logic applies on PC.
0842: 
0843: Xbox can be available.
0844: 
0845: Xbox can be integrated into Windows.
0846: 
0847: Xbox can support console, PC, cloud, mobile, and handheld experiences.
0848: 
0849: But availability across endpoints is not the same as destination value.
0850: 
0851: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0852:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0853:     Xbox can follow the player everywhere without becoming the place the player belongs.
0854:   </p>
0855: </div>
0856: 
0857: That is the missing distinction.
0858: 
0859: Microsoft can unify access across devices.
0860: 
0861: The harder problem is unifying the player's gaming life.
0862: 
0863: Console.
0864: 
0865: PC.
0866: 
0867: Cloud.
0868: 
0869: Mobile.
0870: 
0871: Handheld.
0872: 
0873: Those are entrances.
0874: 
0875: The board is what gives the entrances somewhere to lead.
0876: 
0877: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0878:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0879:     The box can distribute the board. The board can give the box a reason to be chosen.
0880:   </p>
0881: </div>
0882: 
0883: The opportunity is not to make hardware less important.
0884: 
0885: It is to make every endpoint more valuable because it opens onto the same larger environment.
0886: 
0887: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0888:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0889:     X.
0890:   </div>
0891: 
0892:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0893:     When Being the Board Fails
0894:   </h2>
0895: </div>
0896: 
0897: The board is not automatically the winning position.
0898: 
0899: There are several ways the economics can break.
0900: 
0901: ### The Cost Becomes Too High
0902: 
0903: Roblox demonstrates that infrastructure, developer payouts, safety, and operations can consume enormous resources.
0904: 
0905: Scale can create leverage.
0906: 
0907: It can also create scale-dependent costs.
0908: 
0909: ### The Pieces Become Powerful Enough to Leave
0910: 
0911: A sufficiently large publisher may decide that direct distribution, first-party identity, and proprietary commerce are more valuable than participating in a shared board.
0912: 
0913: Network effects do not eliminate bargaining power.
0914: 
0915: They can create it on both sides.
0916: 
0917: ### The Board Becomes Extractive
0918: 
0919: If fees rise unpredictably, rules change without warning, or first-party products receive structural advantages, participants may conclude that independence is safer.
0920: 
0921: The board's leverage can destroy the trust that created the leverage.
0922: 
0923: ### Discovery Can Become Congested
0924: 
0925: A platform containing more things does not automatically make discovery better.
0926: 
0927: More supply can produce noise.
0928: 
0929: If visibility becomes scarce, the board may simply recreate the distribution problems it claimed to solve.
0930: 
0931: ### Network Effects Can Become Lock-In
0932: 
0933: Continuity is valuable.
0934: 
0935: But the same accumulated history, purchases, identity, and relationships that make participation useful can make exit expensive.
0936: 
0937: Earned retention and coercive switching costs are not the same thing.
0938: 
0939: ### Participants May Prefer Modularity
0940: 
0941: Discord, YouTube, Twitch, Steam, independent mod services, and publisher ecosystems demonstrate that specialized systems can create enormous value.
0942: 
0943: A board that tries to absorb everything may destroy the specialization that made those systems useful.
0944: 
0945: That is why the strongest board may not be the most centralized one.
0946: 
0947: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0948:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0949:     XI.
0950:   </div>
0951: 
0952:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0953:     What the Evidence Actually Establishes
0954:   </h2>
0955: </div>
0956: 
0957: The case has to remain narrower than the ambition.
0958: 
0959: The evidence supports several things strongly.
0960: 
0961: Platforms can create economic value by coordinating different participant groups.
0962: 
0963: External innovators can increase the value of a shared industry platform.
0964: 
0965: Fortnite demonstrates a model in which creator success can directly contribute to the health of the platform ecosystem and to the economics of the creators themselves.
0966: 
0967: Roblox demonstrates that shared creation can produce very large economic activity, while also showing that the board itself can be expensive to operate.
0968: 
0969: Epic demonstrates that platform competition can increasingly include alternative economic relationships rather than assuming a fixed 30 percent distribution tax.
0970: 
0971: Microsoft demonstrates that the economic relationship with gaming already extends well beyond the initial hardware transaction, while its current hardware performance cannot tell us what demand would look like under a substantially different platform architecture.
0972: 
0973: What the evidence does not establish is equally important.
0974: 
0975: It does not establish that one unified gaming destination is inevitable.
0976: 
0977: It does not establish that publishers would abandon their own launchers.
0978: 
0979: It does not establish that shared infrastructure always beats vertical integration.
0980: 
0981: It does not establish that Xbox hardware would necessarily gain market share under a board architecture.
0982: 
0983: It does not establish that players migrating from console to PC systematically choose Steam despite having larger Xbox libraries; that remains a plausible and testable migration hypothesis.
0984: 
0985: And it does not establish that Xbox Forever itself would be profitable.
0986: 
0987: Those remain hypotheses.
0988:

0994:   </div>

0997:     Xbox Forever
0998:   </h2>
0999: </div>

1002:

1004:

1006:

1008: 
1009: Its opportunity would be to make those participants more valuable because they operate together.
1010:

1016:

0317: In 2022, Bethesda retired the Bethesda.net Launcher and migrated users' game libraries to Steam. Players could transfer their Bethesda libraries and continue accessing their games through Valve's platform rather than through Bethesda's proprietary launcher.
0318: 
0319: That does not prove proprietary launchers are economically irrational.
0320: 
0321: Bethesda's decision occurred under its own circumstances.
0322: 
0323: But it demonstrates something important:
0324: 
0325: **a publisher can decide that continuing to operate an independent distribution layer is no longer worth the value that layer provides.**
0326: 
0327: That is the strategic threshold a board should attempt to cross.
0328: 
0329: Not through coercion.
0330: 
0331: Through comparative advantage.
0332: 
0333: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0334:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0335:     VI.
0336:   </div>
0337: 
0338:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0339:     Launcher Substitution Value
0340:   </h2>
0341: </div>
0342: 
0343: This creates a useful concept:
0344: 
0345: **launcher substitution value.**
0346: 
0347: The board creates launcher substitution value when participating in the shared environment gives a publisher enough infrastructure, distribution, audience, economics, and control that maintaining a parallel proprietary layer produces diminishing additional benefit.
0348: 
0349: This is not simply a lower store fee.
0350: 
0351: It is a total operating equation.
0352: 
0353: A publisher has to compare:
0354: 
0355: **the value of independence**
0356: 
0357: against
0358: 
0359: **the cost of operating independence**
0360: 
0361: and then compare both against:
0362: 
0363: **the value of participation on the board.**
0364: 
0365: That includes direct economics.
0366: 
0367: But it also includes:
0368: 
0369: development expense,
0370: 
0371: support expense,
0372: 
0373: fraud exposure,
0374: 
0375: compliance expense,
0376: 
0377: technical maintenance,
0378: 
0379: discovery,
0380: 
0381: audience acquisition,
0382: 
0383: payments,
0384: 
0385: social infrastructure,
0386: 
0387: and the opportunity cost of engineering teams maintaining commodity systems rather than differentiated products.
0388: 
0389: The board becomes compelling when the answer becomes:
0390: 
0391: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0392:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0393:     Keep your audience. Keep your identity. Stop rebuilding the infrastructure around them.
0394:   </p>
0395: </div>

0399: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0400:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0401:     VII.
0402:   </div>
0403: 
0404:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0405:     Better Economics Are Necessary, But Not Sufficient
0406:   </h2>
0407: </div>
0408: 
0409: A platform cannot ask publishers to surrender direct economics while claiming to reduce their dependence.
0410: 
0411: The financial proposition has to be competitive.
0412: 
0413: Epic illustrates how high that bar has become.
0414: 
0415: As of 2026, Epic Games Store lets developers retain 100 percent of the first $1 million in annual net revenue per product, after which its standard 88/12 developer-platform split applies. Epic also markets global distribution, more than 80 payment methods, dozens of regional currencies, and access to a large existing user base.
0416: 
0417: That means a future board cannot rely on the old argument that 30 percent is the unavoidable price of distribution.
0418: 
0419: Publishers already have alternatives.
0420: 
0421: The board therefore needs a more sophisticated economic model.
0422: 
0423: One possibility is to separate infrastructure access from transaction fees.
0424: 
0425: Smaller developers may prefer a conventional revenue share because it minimizes fixed cost.
0426: 
0427: Larger publishers may prefer a subscription or negotiated infrastructure contract in exchange for lower marginal transaction fees.
0428: 
0429: A strategic participant may want direct control over portions of its commerce while paying the board for the systems it actually uses.
0430: 
0431: The point is not that one model is correct.
0432: 
0433: The point is that:
0434: 
0435: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0436:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0437:     The economic contract should reflect the infrastructure relationship, not merely impose one universal toll on every participant.
0438:   </p>
0439: </div>
0440: 
0441: That is particularly important if the board wants major publishers and platform operators to participate deeply rather than reluctantly.
0442: 
0443: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0444:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">

0459: fraud,
0460:

0462:

0464: 
0465: identity,
0466:

0468:

0471: privacy,
0472: 
0473: account recovery,
0474: 
0475: marketplace abuse,
0476: 
0477: security,
0478:

0486:

0488: 
0489: permission frameworks,
0490: 
0491: parental controls,
0492: 
0493: transaction standards,
0494: 
0495: fraud detection,

0511: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0512:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0513:     A platform can attract participants not only by offering distribution, but by reducing the cost of being a responsible participant.
0514:   </p>
0515: </div>
0516: 
0517: The board does not remove responsibility from publishers.
0518: 
0519: It can make responsible operation easier.
0520:

0522: 
0523: And an important one.

0525: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0526:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0527:     IX.
0528:   </div>
0529: 
0530:   <h2 className="text-3xl font-semibold tracking-tight text-white">
0531:     Responsibility Should Follow Control
0532:   </h2>
0533: </div>
0534: 
0535: Shared governance also creates a danger.
0536: 
0537: If the board becomes involved everywhere, does it become responsible for everything?
0538: 
0539: Not necessarily.
0540: 
0541: A more useful principle is:
0542: 
0543: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0544:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0545:     Responsibility should follow control.
0546:   </p>
0547: </div>
0548: 
0549: If the board operates the payment layer, it owns responsibility for the payment systems it controls.
0550: 
0551: If it operates shared identity, it owns responsibility for that identity layer.
0552: 
0553: If it defines platform-wide marketplace rules, it owns responsibility for those rules.
0554: 
0555: But a developer that controls its game's internal content, economy, design, or community decisions retains responsibility for those systems.
0556: 
0557: The boundaries will never be perfectly clean.
0558: 
0559: Platforms influence participants through interfaces, incentives, rules, access, and technical standards. A systematic review of 103 platform-governance studies describes governance precisely as the mechanisms platform owners use to influence complementors and users and sustain an ecosystem.
0560: 
0561: So the board cannot pretend to be neutral infrastructure while exercising meaningful control.
0562: 
0563: But neither must every responsibility collapse upward into the board.
0564: 
0565: This leads to a stronger architecture:
0566: 
0567: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0568:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0569:     Sovereign pieces. Shared standards. Shared protections. Bounded board authority.
0570:   </p>
0571: </div>
0572: 
0573: The board governs the relationships it creates.
0574: 
0575: The pieces govern the domains they control.
0576: 
0577: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0578:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0579:     X.
0580:   </div>
0581:

0583:     Make Good Governance Easier Than Bad Governance
0584:   </h2>
0585: </div>
0586: 
0587: A well-designed board should create incentives through architecture.
0588: 
0589: Not merely policy documents.
0590: 
0591: If safe payments are easier to implement than unsafe ones, more participants will use them.
0592: 
0593: If parental controls are available through common infrastructure, developers do not need to rebuild them from zero.
0594: 
0595: If identity permissions are standardized, developers have less reason to invent inconsistent access systems.
0596: 
0597: If fraud protection is available at the board level, smaller publishers gain capabilities that may otherwise be expensive to operate independently.
0598: 
0599: The goal is not to eliminate bad behavior through architecture.
0600: 
0601: That is unrealistic.
0602: 
0603: The goal is to make responsible participation the path of least resistance.
0604: 
0605: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0606:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0607:     The board can make good governance easier than bad governance.
0608:   </p>
0609: </div>

0611: That creates value for players.
0612:

0614:

0618: 
0619: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0620:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">

0625:     Interoperability Is Not a Concession
0626:   </h2>
0627: </div>
0628: 
0629: Platform owners often treat interoperability as something they are forced to provide.
0630:

The Commission explicitly connects those requirements to network effects, user lock-in, business-user dependence, and the competitive power of large platforms.
0636: 
0637: The policy debate around those rules is separate from the architectural point.
0638: 
0639: The important signal is that data portability, access, and interoperability have become central questions in modern platform design.
0640: 
0641: A future board can treat those capabilities as strategic advantages rather than regulatory burdens.
0642: 
0643: A participant should be able to know:
0644: 
0645: what data it controls,
0646: 
0647: what data the board controls,
0648: 
0649: how identity travels,
0650: 
0651: how relationships are represented,
0652: 
0653: how commerce works,
0654: 
0655: what happens if it leaves,
0656: 
0657: and what remains portable.
0658: 
0659: That matters because participants will hesitate to build deeply on infrastructure they believe can later trap them.
0660: 
0661: Openness can therefore create trust.
0662: 
0663: But complete openness can also undermine coherence, safety, and operating discipline.
0664: 
0665: The design problem is not:
0666: 
0667: **open or closed.**
0668: 
0669: It is:
0670: 
0671: <div className="not-prose my-20 max-w-4xl">
0672:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
0673:     What should be shared, what should remain sovereign, and where should the boundary live?
0674:   </p>
0675: </div>
0676: 
0677: That is the real governance question.
0678: 
0679: <div className="not-prose mt-24 mb-8">
0680:   <div className="text-base tracking-[0.2em] uppercase text-gray-500 mb-3">
0681:     XII.
0682:   </div>

0687: </div>

0694: 
0695: Fees can rise.

0712:

0716:

0771: The healthier model is different.
0772: 
0773: A publisher joins because it gains access to capabilities that would be inefficient to reproduce alone.
0774: 
0775: A developer reaches more players.
0776: 
0777: A creator reaches more communities.
0778:

0788:   <p className="text-2xl md:text-3xl leading-relaxed font-medium text-white">
